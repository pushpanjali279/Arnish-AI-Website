Claim automation

How Insurance Claim Processing Automation Works in India

Insurance claim processing automation uses software for the clerical steps of a health claim in India: intake, document checks, data extraction, policy rule checks and status messages. Medical and coverage decisions stay with trained reviewers. Arnish AI, a software company based in Shirpur, Maharashtra, builds these systems for insurers, TPAs and hospitals.

By the Arnish AI teamPublished 3 November 202610 min read

What does claim automation change in a health claim?

Software takes over the typing and checking, and the decision stays with the processor or doctor. It reads documents, fills fields and runs rule checks, so a reviewer opens a claim that is already organised.

A health claim in India passes through several hands: the hospital insurance desk, a processor at the TPA or insurer, a medical reviewer, and the finance team that releases payment. At each handoff, someone re-keys data, chases a missing paper or checks a limit by hand.

The two claim routes put pressure on different things. In a cashless claim, the network hospital sends a pre-authorisation request and the insurer or TPA responds while the patient is still admitted, so speed matters most. In a reimbursement claim, the policyholder pays first and claims later, so accuracy and avoiding repeat document requests matter more.

The stages below are the same ones we map when scoping claim automation software.

Which claim stages can software handle, and which need a reviewer?

Software can do most of the preparation at every stage. People should keep the judgement calls: medical necessity, unclear policy wording, rejections and complaints.

Intake and document collection

Claims arrive as portal uploads, emailed scans and, where NHCX is in use, structured messages. Software can turn all of these into a single claim record, match it to the policy and member, and compare the attached documents against a checklist for that procedure.

A missing-document check run at intake lets the TPA ask the hospital once, with a complete list, instead of sending queries one at a time. For a planned surgery, that list might cover the pre-authorisation form, investigation reports, the final bill and the discharge summary.

Data extraction

OCR and document models can read printed hospital bills and discharge summaries into structured fields such as dates of admission, diagnosis text, line items and totals. The output should carry a confidence score for each field.

A person should confirm fields below the agreed confidence level, because one wrong digit in a bill total flows through every later check.

Policy and coverage checks

Most coverage checks are arithmetic and date comparisons, which software handles reliably once the policy terms are stored as data: sum insured remaining, room rent eligibility, disease-wise sub-limits, waiting periods and co-payment percentages.

Reviewers should keep the cases where policy wording is open to interpretation, or where a product has an exception the rules do not yet describe. When a rule cannot decide, the system should say so and route the claim, not guess.

Medical coding and review

Software can suggest diagnosis codes from the WHO International Classification of Diseases, compare the treatment billed against the diagnosis, and flag a stay that is long for the procedure or a line item that looks out of place.

Medical necessity is a clinical judgement and belongs with a qualified doctor. The software's job is to put the discharge summary, the codes and the flags on one screen so that review takes less searching.

Adjudication and settlement

Once checks are done, the system can calculate the payable amount and show each deduction next to the policy clause behind it. That gives the adjudicator a draft decision to accept or change.

Rejections need extra care. Software can assemble the file a senior reviewer needs, with the documents, the rules that fired and the clauses involved, but a person decides. On the payment side, software can build payment files and reconcile them, while release of large amounts stays with finance.

Communication

Status messages to hospitals and policyholders can go out as soon as a claim changes stage. Deduction letters can be drafted from the adjudication data, so each one quotes the clause used.

Complaints and appeals should go to a person who can reopen the file and explain the deduction.

Where does document extraction usually fail?

Extraction tends to fail on unreadable scans, bills split across several files, varied hospital bill layouts, handwriting, and totals that do not match their line items. Each needs its own check, because a better OCR model fixes only some of them.

Poor scans. Phone photos taken at an angle, shadows across the page and pages cropped at the bottom edge, where the bill total often sits. A quality check at upload can flag these at once, so the hospital desk rescans the page while the file is still in hand.

Mixed and split files. One PDF may hold the bill, discharge summary and lab reports in no particular order, while another claim spreads one bill across three uploads. The system should label each page by type and group pages of the same document before reading any field.

Bill layouts and handwriting. Each hospital billing system prints differently, with wrapped line items, package rates on a single row, and discounts below the total. Build templates for the hospitals that send the most claims and use a general model for the rest. Handwritten case sheets and notes should always go to a person.

Arithmetic and matching errors. Line items that do not add up to the total, mixed date formats, and a patient name spelled differently on the ID card and the bill. Summing line items, checking dates against the admission period and sending near-miss name matches to a reviewer catch most of these.

Why does data quality decide whether automation works?

Every rule in a claim system acts on extracted data. If a member ID, admission date or bill total is wrong, every check after it gives a confident wrong answer.

Beyond the document problems above, errors come from policy terms stored as free text and member records that differ between insurer and TPA. Better OCR fixes neither.

Before building, we ask for a sample of real claim files and check how many fields can be read cleanly, which hospital formats recur, and where policy terms live. The answer decides the order of work. If policy terms sit in PDFs and spreadsheets, turning them into structured tables may need to come before any extraction model, because the rule checks cannot run without them.

How do you keep automated claim decisions auditable?

Record every step: what the software read, which rule it applied, which version of that rule, and what the reviewer changed. An auditor or a senior reviewer should be able to replay any claim.

In practice, this needs a few design choices made early. Rules should live in versioned tables, not buried in code, so you can show which rule was active on the day of a decision. Each extracted field should keep a link to the page and position it came from. Reviewer overrides should be stored with a reason.

When reviewers keep overriding the same rule, the log shows it, and the rule can be corrected.

Where does NHCX fit in claim automation?

The National Health Claims Exchange (NHCX) is a claims exchange run by the National Health Authority for passing claim information between hospitals, insurers and TPAs. It carries claims as structured data instead of scanned paper.

NHCX uses FHIR R4 profiles published by NRCeS. Pre-authorisation and claim requests travel as a claim bundle, and there are separate bundles for eligibility checks and claim responses.

For automation, structured claims reduce the OCR burden for those requests. Scanned documents will still arrive for some time, so a claim system needs to handle both, mapping each into the same internal record.

How should claim software protect patient data?

Treat every claim file as sensitive by default. Give each person access only to the claims they work on, log who opened what, encrypt stored documents, and decide early where any AI or OCR model runs and who can see its inputs.

Encrypt claim documents at rest and in transit, and keep them only as long as your retention policy allows. Before pages go to an external AI or OCR service, ask where it processes data, whether it keeps copies, and whether the task can run on servers you control.

What should you measure before and after a pilot?

Measure handling time per stage, the share of claims needing a document query, first-pass extraction accuracy, and how often reviewers override the software. Record the same numbers for the same claim type before and after, counted the same way, so the comparison is fair.

Take the baseline from a few weeks of real claims, not estimates. Keep the counting method fixed for both periods, and report by hospital, shift and reviewer so problems show up during the pilot. We suggest pairing a pilot with claim analytics dashboards for this.

How should an insurer, TPA or hospital start?

Pick one claim type, measure how it is handled today, and run a pilot against that baseline. A narrow start gives you real numbers sooner and limits the risk of a wrong rule affecting many claims.

Choose a claim type with steady volume and fairly standard documents, such as a common planned surgery under cashless. Then record the baseline measures above for that claim type.

Run the pilot with software doing the preparation and reviewers keeping every decision. Compare the same measures after a fixed period. If the numbers improve and reviewers trust the output, widen the scope to the next claim type.

Frequently asked questions

Can insurance claim processing automation approve claims without a person?

Software can approve narrow, low-risk claim categories if the insurer writes rules for them, but we do not suggest starting there. Rejections and partial disallowances need a reviewer who can read the file and explain the outcome. Begin by automating preparation, then widen the rules as the accuracy record builds.

How accurate is OCR on Indian hospital bills?

It depends on scan quality, bill layout and how much is handwritten, so we measure it on your own documents during a pilot instead of quoting a general figure. Printed bills from hospital billing systems tend to extract well. Handwritten notes and phone photos of pages need more human confirmation.

Do we need to join NHCX before automating claims?

No. Document extraction, policy checks and reviewer queues work whether claims arrive through NHCX, a hospital portal or email. It is sensible to design the claim record so it maps to the NHCX FHIR profiles, because that makes a later connection much simpler.

Is claim automation useful for hospitals, or only for insurers and TPAs?

Hospitals gain from the same ideas on their side of the claim. Software can check a pre-authorisation request for missing documents before it is sent and show how long each insurer query has been open. That reduces the back-and-forth that delays cashless approvals and discharges.

How long does a claim automation pilot take?

It depends on the claim type and how many systems need to connect. We scope a pilot around one claim type and a fixed measurement period, agree the baseline numbers before building, and decide on a wider rollout only after comparing results with that baseline.
