How to Choose a Custom AI Software Development Company in India

Judge a custom AI software development company in India on how it treats your data, how it measures accuracy on your own documents and what happens when the AI gets something wrong. Arnish AI, a software company in Shirpur, Maharashtra, builds custom AI for insurers, hospitals and businesses. These are the questions we would ask any vendor.

By the Arnish AI teamPublished 3 November 202610 min read

AI projects often disappoint for ordinary reasons. The vendor did not understand the work, accuracy was not measured on real documents, or the contract left the buyer without the code. A buyer can check each of these before signing.

When does custom AI make more sense than an off-the-shelf tool?

Build custom AI when your documents, rules or systems are specific enough that a general product would need heavy workarounds. Buy an off-the-shelf tool when your need is common and the tool already handles it well.

A general AI assistant will not know that your insurer clients apply different room rent sub-limits, or that your hospital's discharge summaries follow a template your doctors changed last year.

Custom work tends to pay off when these conditions hold:

  • The task repeats often enough that small time savings per case add up.
  • The inputs are your own documents, in formats a generic product has not seen.
  • The output has to land in a system you already run, such as a claims platform or hospital information system.
  • Mistakes carry a cost, so you need control over how errors are caught.

If none of these apply, try a packaged product first. A good vendor will tell you so. We include that advice in our IT consulting work, because a clear buy-or-build answer serves a buyer better than a project that should not exist.

Why does domain knowledge matter when choosing an AI vendor?

A vendor that knows your industry asks about exceptions in the first meeting and builds checks for them before launch. In healthcare and insurance, those details decide whether the software is usable.

Take a health claim. A team new to the field might treat it as a form to read. A team that knows the work will ask whether the claim is cashless or reimbursement, which documents each insurer requires, how co-payments and sub-limits apply, and which fields a medical reviewer must see before deciding. Those questions shape the data model, the screens and the error checks.

To test domain knowledge, describe one real case from your operations and ask the vendor to walk through how their software would handle it. Listen for whether they ask about exceptions. What should you ask an AI vendor about handling your data?

Ask where your data will be stored, who can see it, how it is protected and how it will be deleted, and get each answer in writing. A vendor that will handle patient records or claim files should be able to answer these questions in the first few meetings.

Questions to ask about data handling

  • Which data do you need for the build, and can identifiers be masked in test samples?
  • Where will our data be stored and processed, in which country and cloud region?
  • Who on your team can see our data, and how is that access removed when someone leaves the project?
  • Is our data encrypted in transit and at rest, and who holds the encryption keys?
  • Do you send our data to a third-party AI model provider? If so, which one, in which region, and does that provider keep or train on it?
  • Do you keep audit logs of who opened, changed or exported our data, and can we see them on request?
  • How and how fast will you tell us if our data is exposed or mishandled?
  • How do you delete our data at the end of the contract, including backups and copies held by subcontractors, and will you confirm the deletion in writing?

Ask for these commitments as written data-processing terms attached to the contract, and have your own legal adviser review them before you sign.

Many AI features call an external language model. For such software, the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications lists security risks such as prompt injection and sensitive information disclosure. Ask the vendor which apply to your system and how it tests for them.

Who should own the code, models and prompts?

You should own, or hold a lasting licence to, everything you need to run and change the software without the original vendor. Settle this in the contract before work starts.

With AI software, "the code" covers more than application source. List these items in the agreement:

  • Application source code and deployment scripts
  • Prompts, extraction rules and configuration files
  • Fine-tuned model weights, if any model is trained on your data
  • The labelled test sets built from your documents
  • Documentation good enough for another team to take over

Vendors often reuse internal libraries across projects, and that is reasonable. Ask for a perpetual licence to those parts so that a change of vendor does not stop your system. If a vendor will not hand over the prompts or test sets, you will struggle to measure or improve the system later.

How should a vendor test AI accuracy on your own documents?

A vendor should measure accuracy on a set of your real documents that the system has not seen during development, field by field, and share the results before launch. Accuracy on a demo file tells you little.

A sound testing plan usually looks like this:

  1. You and the vendor agree on a sample of documents that reflects real variety, including poor scans, handwritten notes and unusual formats.
  2. Your staff label the correct answers for each field. This becomes the test set.
  3. Part of the set is held back and not used while building, so the final score is not inflated.
  4. Results are reported per field, because a system can read patient names well and misread bill totals.
  5. The vendor shows you the errors themselves, so you can judge which ones matter.

Ask how the system signals uncertainty. Many extraction setups can attach a confidence level to each field, which lets low-confidence fields go to a person. Ask too how the vendor will re-run the test set after each change, including when an external model provider updates its model.

What does good human review design look like?

Good review design sends uncertain or high-stakes cases to a person, shows that person the source document beside the extracted data, and records every correction. The aim is for people to make the decisions that need judgement while the software handles typing and checking.

In claims work, we design so that the software prepares the file and a reviewer decides. A field the system is unsure about is highlighted, and the reviewer can click through to the exact place in the scanned bill it came from. Corrections are saved so the test set grows and repeated errors become visible.

Ask a vendor to show you the reviewer screen early. If the plan has no review step at all for a task that affects money or health, treat that as a warning. We design AI applications and chatbots this way, with people kept in charge of the final call.

Fixed scope or time-and-materials: which pricing model fits?

Fixed scope suits work you can describe in detail upfront. Time-and-materials suits work where accuracy has to be discovered on your data, which is common in AI projects. Many buyers use a mix.

ModelWorks well whenWatch for
Fixed scopeRequirements, screens and integrations are clear and stableVendors padding the price for risk, or disputes over what counts as a change
Time-and-materialsAccuracy targets and edge cases will only become clear during testingOpen-ended spend without regular reports on hours and progress
Phased mixA short discovery or pilot phase comes first, then a fixed price for the defined buildA pilot with no end date or written result

Whichever model you choose, ask for a written scope that says what is excluded, how changes are priced and what the acceptance test is. Ask separately about running costs. AI software often has usage charges for model calls and hosting that continue after the build is paid for.

What support should you expect after launch?

Expect the vendor to monitor accuracy, fix defects within agreed times and handle changes in the documents or models the system depends on.

Accuracy can drift. An insurer changes a claim form, a hospital switches billing software, or a model provider retires the version your system uses. Each can lower accuracy without any error message. A support agreement should name who watches for this, how often the test set is re-run and how quickly fixes are made.

Your staff should know how to report a wrong output and see what happened to it. Ask any vendor, including us, to write training and post-launch fixes into the support agreement. Our custom software and SaaS service scopes these before the build starts.

What are the red flags when hiring an AI development company?

The clearest red flag is an accuracy promise made before the vendor has seen your documents. A vendor cannot know how well a system will read your files until it has been tested on them.

Other signs to take seriously:

  • No plan for errors: the proposal describes what the AI does when it is right, and says nothing about catching it when it is wrong.
  • Vague answers on where your data goes, or reluctance to name third-party model providers.
  • A contract that keeps prompts, model weights or test sets with the vendor.
  • No acceptance test, or one based on the vendor's own sample files.
  • Pressure to automate decisions that affect patients or payouts with no human review.
  • A large upfront commitment with no smaller first phase to prove the approach.

Each of these leaves the risk with you unless the gap is closed in writing.

To compare vendors in a structured way, use the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which groups AI risk work into four functions: govern, map, measure and manage. Ask each vendor how its process covers them.

What does a good first AI project look like?

A good first project covers one workflow, uses documents you already have in volume, has a measurable accuracy target and keeps a person reviewing the output. It should be small enough to finish and judge within a single budget cycle.

For an insurer or TPA, that might be extracting fields from one document type, such as hospital bills, and sending low-confidence fields to a processor. For a hospital group, it could be checking pre-authorization requests for missing documents before they are sent. For an SME, a chatbot that answers order or policy questions from your own knowledge base, with handover to staff, is a contained start.

When we scope a first project, we ask what the team does today, how many cases pass through the step, what a mistake costs and who will review the output. The answers decide the accuracy target and the review design. A first project that ends with a clear test report gives you the evidence to decide whether to expand.

Frequently asked questions

How much does custom AI software development cost in India?

Cost depends on how many document types or workflows the software covers, how many systems it must connect to and how much testing your accuracy targets need. A first project limited to one workflow costs far less than a full platform. Ask any vendor for a written scope and a separate price for the first phase before you commit to the whole build.

Who owns the AI model when a vendor builds it for us?

Ownership is whatever the contract says, so put it in writing. Ask for the source code, prompts, configuration, any fine-tuned model weights and the test sets built from your documents. If the vendor reuses its own libraries, ask for a licence that lets you keep running and changing the software if you part ways.

Does an AI vendor need our real customer or patient data?

To measure accuracy properly, the vendor needs samples that look like your real documents. That does not mean full access to your database. A careful vendor asks for a limited, agreed sample, masks identifiers where the task allows it, keeps the data in an agreed location and deletes it when the contract ends.

What should our contract with an AI vendor say about data?

It should say where your data is stored, who at the vendor can access it, how it is encrypted, whether it goes to any external AI model provider, how incidents are reported to you and how the data is deleted when the contract ends. Put these terms in writing and have your own legal adviser review them before signing.
